Comparing AI Integration Strategies in Education

Three studies explore different approaches to integrating AI in education, from a staged introduction in formal settings to gradual normalization in higher education. Meanwhile, a systematic review and a writing study highlight the psychological and practical impacts of AI, raising questions about long-term engagement and ethical concerns.

In the first study, an essay proposes a staged approach to integrating AI in formal education, suggesting that AI support should follow demonstrated learning readiness. In contrast, a study in higher education found that gradual normalization of AI use led to increased uptake and positive attitudes, but raised ethical concerns. Another study examined the effects of prompt-based emotional scaffolding versus general-purpose chatbot interaction, finding that emotional support improved enjoyment and task-level performance. However, a systematic review highlighted the psychological processes underlying GenAI interactions, while a writing study found that AI-supported writing did not correlate with strong independent performance. These studies raise questions about the long-term impacts and ethical considerations of AI in education.

The second source describes a study in higher education where students gradually normalized AI use, leading to increased uptake and more positive attitudes, but raised ethical concerns about the authenticity of learning. Both approaches aim to integrate AI, but the first is more cautious, advocating for a gradual introduction based on readiness, while the second suggests a natural integration over time, though with ongoing ethical scrutiny.

The study in S2 found that while all groups improved on immediate conceptual-knowledge and learning-transfer tests, the emotional SRLbot showed larger improvements in enjoyment and reductions in boredom compared to the general-purpose ChatGPT group. It also demonstrated higher task-level performance and reflection indicators. In contrast, S6 showed that generative AI technostress was negatively associated with both learning engagement and academic adaptability, suggesting that technostress hinders these outcomes. Both studies highlight the importance of emotional support and adaptability in learning, but S2's one-time intervention and S6's cross-sectional design limit the conclusions about long-term effects and causal relationships.
