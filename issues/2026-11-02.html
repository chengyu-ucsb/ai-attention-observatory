When AI helps, what do we still do ourselves?

Checking an AI answer, solving a problem without it, and choosing what it can access are different kinds of control. This week’s readings make those differences concrete, from students asking for math help to phone owners protecting their messages.

It is easy to call someone an active AI user.

The opening argument

It is easy to call someone an active AI user. It is harder to say what they are actually doing: checking a claim, working through a problem, or simply keeping a conversation going. This week, start with those actions. Two studies compare students’ accounts or chat behavior with different outcomes. A prototype study asks what makes a chatbot feel like a classmate. Two EFF essays tackle a separate, practical problem: how people can limit or challenge an automated system. The useful connections are specific, not a single verdict on whether AI helps us think.

Theme 01

Saying you checked an answer is different from solving the next problem

In Akgun and Toker’s survey of 891 college students in the US and Türkiye, students who reported coordinating work with AI and reworking its suggestions were also more likely to report thinking critically about its output. Their critical-thinking measure was a yes/no answer, followed by a checklist of tactics such as checking AI output against other sources. Nobody took a critical-thinking test. The survey connects students’ accounts of related habits; it does not show that AI improved their ability.

Abdelghani, Kaiser and Murayama took a different approach: they observed 112 ninth-graders in three German schools practicing math with Mistral, a general-purpose chatbot, then tested them without AI. Their analysis of 89 students linked a shift toward requests for explanations or methods with better immediate test scores, after accounting for initial scores and other chat measures. On average, though, requests shifted in the opposite direction. The promising clue is how a conversation changes, rather than a count of “good” prompts. The paper’s text and table report different p-values for the same model comparison. The discrepancy does not reverse the authors’ conclusion, but it needs clarification. And observation alone cannot show that changing someone’s prompts would improve their learning.

Theme 02

A chatbot can feel friendly while getting the math wrong

In Lin and colleagues’ Exploring AI Peer in Collaborative Mathematics Learning, seven researchers spent about 20 minutes imagining themselves as students solving equations with a chatbot. In interviews, they described feeling less judged than with a human classmate. But collaboration broke down when the bot rushed to an answer, took over the pace, or agreed too readily. Mistakes made it feel more like a peer to some participants and less useful to others.

One illustrated exchange makes the problem tangible: for x² + 5x − 6 = 0, the bot asks for numbers that multiply to +6, when they should multiply to −6. This is an error in a depicted example, not a measured error rate. The interviews tell us what these adults liked or resisted; they do not tell us whether students learned. Set beside Abdelghani and colleagues’ math study, the design question becomes sharper: can a chatbot leave room for a learner’s ideas, help them notice errors, and still prepare them to solve a problem alone? That is a question to test, not a benefit either study has established.

Theme 03

Control also means being able to say no, or ask for another look

EFF’s guide to excluding apps from Siri AI gives a concrete example of setting a boundary before a system acts. EFF reports that excluding Messages removes Siri’s ability to read and reply to messages. The loss of convenience is part of the trade-off: a meaningful restriction should stop the assistant doing something. This is EFF’s account of the settings, not our own software or security test.

EFF’s essay on election moderation tackles what happens after a system acts. When a platform removes a post, the writer needs a way to explain context an automated filter may have missed. EFF argues for appeals to human reviewers, clearer disclosure and oversight. These are policy proposals, not measured proof that the safeguards work. Unlike the learning studies, these essays are about permission and recourse: who can prevent an action, and who can challenge it afterwards. A tool that helps someone think and a tool they can refuse need different kinds of evaluation.

The productive tension

What should we ask a learning tool to leave behind?

A useful next test would connect the experience of using a chatbot with what a learner can do later. Lin and colleagues’ interviews suggest choices worth comparing, such as letting the learner set the pace rather than jumping ahead. Abdelghani and colleagues’ study points to a way of checking the result: look at the conversation, then assess the learner without AI. A follow-up could compare specific designs and repeat the assessment after a delay. That would help distinguish a pleasant interaction, immediate success and learning that lasts. It would still be a learning test, not an answer to EFF’s separate questions about privacy or appeal rights.

A few useful terms

preprints
Research manuscripts shared publicly, usually before formal review by other researchers is complete. The review status of these three papers is unverified.
p-values
A low p-value means results at least this extreme would be unusual if there were no relationship and the statistical model’s assumptions held. It does not measure importance or prove cause and effect.
recourse
A way to challenge a decision and seek a correction, such as asking a human to review a removed post.
