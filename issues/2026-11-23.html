Who did the thinking?

A polished answer can hide who evaluated the evidence. This week’s research, classroom reporting and workplace guidance point to a question worth asking. Which decisions did the person make, and could they explain them again?

Imagine grading an excellent case analysis.

The opening argument

Imagine grading an excellent case analysis. The student submitted a complete chat history alongside it. You can see what the assistant suggested, but how would you tell which ideas the student challenged or why they chose the final explanation? That gap matters to anyone trying to assess what someone learned, from a teacher reading a draft to a supervisor reviewing a new employee’s work.

Theme 01

Which decisions did the student make?

Li and colleagues reviewed 93 higher-education studies. Most described what students handed over to AI. Only four documented both how students handled the output and what they could do on a separate task without AI. One student’s decision to restore their own terminology after reviewing an AI edit reveals a judgment that the final wording alone could never show.

Kearney’s worked example applies that distinction to a case analysis. Asking students to explain which objections they accepted or rejected gives a teacher evidence about their reasoning. Austin and colleagues propose a related biochemistry exercise in which students annotate the assumptions in an AI explanation. Both papers offer assessment designs whose learning effects still need testing.

Theme 02

The same tool can save effort and demand it

A review by Wang and colleagues modeled two associations between AI use and critical thinking across 31 studies. More use was linked with stronger learning motivation, but also with dependence. The positive and negative indirect associations nearly canceled. In Jia’s survey of 1,050 university students, people who said they delegated thinking to AI reported different kinds of effort, including checking its output. The survey found no clear overall linear relationship between offloading and engagement.

Su and colleagues found that Chinese adolescents who reported greater AI dependence also reported greater difficulty directing their attention and earned lower scores on standardized school tests. All measures came from the same wave. The study leaves open whether dependence preceded attention difficulties, whether students who struggled already relied more on AI, or whether other factors contributed to both.

Theme 03

Keeping a first attempt visible takes time

Colorado Public Radio interviewed students using AI in quite different ways. One asked for revision advice on her own essay using the teacher’s feedback and rubric. Another used AI for mechanics while keeping the voice of his college essay his own. A Denver professor moved writing into class to see the work unfold, leaving less time for other instruction.

In EdSurge, seventh-grade literacy teacher Court Shuller describes keeping first drafts on paper and discussing ideas aloud. Together, these accounts identify the time it takes to make thinking visible. Someone has to be there to watch, listen and respond. Neither article measured whether students learned more. A small German university study illustrates why activity logs also need interpretation. Students launched a GPT exam assistant 224 times, but only about half those sessions included an answer to the first question.

The productive tension

Disclosure tells a reader less than the decisions behind it

Matsui asks what a label such as AI-assisted editing actually tells a journal reader. A suggested objection might alter an author’s reasoning even if its wording disappears. The paper proposes testing whether disclosure draws attention away from the evidence. It reports no experiment showing that distraction occurs.

The American Psychological Association brings a similar question to work design. If employees must verify AI suggestions, managers need to budget time for that work and preserve opportunities to practice the underlying skills. A disclosure tells us that a tool was involved. Knowing who could catch an error, and when they had the chance to do so, tells us more about accountability.
