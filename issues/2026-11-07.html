Where judgment remains when systems assist

The available readings distinguish checking an answer, performing without assistance, and setting boundaries on automated systems. They offer different ways to ask who retains control, but no common demonstration that assistance strengthens independent judgment.

A useful thread through this collection is the difference between having help and retaining a decision.

Read the opening argument

A useful thread through this collection is the difference between having help and retaining a decision. The research readings consider reported evaluation of AI output, performance after mathematics practice, and reactions to a prototype companion. The commentary shifts the question to control over application access and moderation decisions. Those are distinct settings and kinds of evidence, not a shared test of autonomy. The preprints have unverified review status and substantial design or reporting limits; the EFF pieces make attributed arguments. Read together, they support a more precise editorial question: what would count as retained judgment in each setting, and what would have to be measured before claiming it?

Signal 01

Reported engagement and independent performance

The critical-engagement preprint uses a single student survey, whereas the mathematics-interaction preprint relates practice conversations to an immediate test taken without AI.

Read the full analysis

The critical-engagement preprint uses a single student survey, whereas the mathematics-interaction preprint relates practice conversations to an immediate test taken without AI. In the survey, reported coordination and reflection were associated with a reported act of critical engagement. In the classroom account, shifts toward conceptual or procedural help were associated with test performance, although reporting inconsistencies make that result uncertain. Neither observation shows that AI improves critical-thinking skill. For both preprints, peer review is unverified and the evidence does not establish causality. Their limits differ: the survey relies on overlapping self-reports and exploratory model choices; the classroom analysis follows one session and contains unresolved statistical and coding-reporting discrepancies. These outcomes should remain separate, rather than become a single story about learning gains.

Signal 02

A conversation is not a learning outcome

The classroom preprint and prototype preprint ask different questions about mathematics conversations.

Read the full analysis

The classroom preprint and prototype preprint ask different questions about mathematics conversations. In the classroom account, the authors propose examining how requests unfold instead of grading isolated prompts as good or bad. The prototype work interviewed researchers who acted as students, so its accounts of pacing, uncertainty and shared control describe an exploratory role-play. Participants described problems with premature answers and automatic agreement, but the work did not assess student learning or compare alternative designs. Both sources are preprints; peer review is unverified, and this evidence does not establish causality. Unresolved reporting discrepancies limit the classroom result. Mathematical errors in depicted prototype exchanges also caution against treating the prototype as verified instruction. A productive next question is whether these interaction features matter when actual learners work independently.

Signal 03

Different ways to limit automated decisions

EFF's Siri commentary welcomes excluding selected apps from assistant access and argues for simpler controls.

Read the full analysis

EFF's Siri commentary welcomes excluding selected apps from assistant access and argues for simpler controls. Its election-moderation essay instead emphasizes what happens after an automated decision, proposing human appeals, clearer disclosure and oversight. The comparison concerns different forms of control: limiting access in advance and challenging a decision afterwards. EFF also describes trade-offs, including lost assistant features and the difficulty of interpreting context at platform scale. These are attributed policy and product arguments, not evidence that those arrangements work as intended. The readings do not independently test software settings, audit security or demonstrate the effectiveness of moderation safeguards. Their contemporary HTML was checked without a verified historical snapshot, so the account should not imply exact reconstruction of either page during publication week.

The productive tension

What would retained judgment look like in each setting?

How could future evaluations distinguish the opportunity to exercise judgment from evidence that people actually do so?

Explore the tension

How could future evaluations distinguish the opportunity to exercise judgment from evidence that people actually do so? The survey, classroom conversations and prototype interviews do not measure the same outcome. Their preprint status also matters: peer review is unverified, and this evidence does not establish causality. EFF's commentary offers proposals about boundaries and recourse, not a test that settles the research question. An evaluation would need to specify whose decision is retained, when assistance is absent, and which outcome is being assessed. That is an editorial question suggested by these contrasts, not a finding that one intervention works across learning, privacy and public discussion.

Try it

Keep one decision visible

Before using AI on a task, write down one judgment you want to make yourself. Afterwards, note what evidence changed that judgment.

A reflection prompt, not a tested intervention.

Which observable decision would show that a learner or user retained judgment, and how would that observation differ from a favorable report about the system?
