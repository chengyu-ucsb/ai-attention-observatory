What AI use tells us about learning

This week’s clearest distinction is between wanting to use AI, feeling that learning is going well, and experiencing the costs of that use. Those outcomes call for different evidence and different questions about attention.

An appealing AI tool can fit into a learner’s routine without telling us whether it improves independent learning.

Read the opening argument

An appealing AI tool can fit into a learner’s routine without telling us whether it improves independent learning. This week’s selected readings make that distinction useful: one examines willingness to use AI, another connects reported dependency with persistence and perceived learning, and a third explores adult learners’ wellbeing. Read together, they suggest an evaluation agenda, rather than a shared verdict on AI. Ask what people are willing to try, what effort they retain, and what the experience costs them. Then measure those outcomes separately.

Signal 01

Ease and confidence are questions about uptake

Isaac Davor and colleagues surveyed university students in Ghana about their intention to use generative AI.

Read the full analysis

Isaac Davor and colleagues surveyed university students in Ghana about their intention to use generative AI. Students who expected the tools to be useful and easy to use reported greater willingness to use them; anxiety about digital learning was associated with lower willingness. These were responses collected at one point in time.

The implication is a distinction to preserve when evaluating a tool: an inviting interface and a reassuring experience concern uptake. They do not, by themselves, demonstrate better learning. This survey identifies associations with intention, not the effects of an intervention on knowledge, performance, or sustained attention.

Signal 02

Look beyond how often a tool is used

ShuFei Li and colleagues examined students’ reported AI dependency, ability to sustain effort, and perceptions of their learning.

Read the full analysis

ShuFei Li and colleagues examined students’ reported AI dependency, ability to sustain effort, and perceptions of their learning. Greater dependency was associated with less persistence and a small negative overall association with perceived learning. The remaining direct association, after accounting for effort regulation, was not statistically distinguishable from zero. Those are different estimates, not contradictory conclusions.

This was a cross-sectional survey, so the pattern cannot establish what caused what. The outcomes were learners’ assessments, not demonstrated achievement. Unaccounted classroom clustering also limits confidence in the small overall association. For evaluation, the useful question is whether learners keep working through difficulty, rather than treating frequency of AI use as a sufficient measure of dependence.

Signal 03

A positive average can conceal individual strain

Michael Agyemang Adarkwah and colleagues combined a survey with accounts from adult learners at a Ghanaian university.

Read the full analysis

Michael Agyemang Adarkwah and colleagues combined a survey with accounts from adult learners at a Ghanaian university. The survey’s overall picture of digital wellbeing was positive, but some respondents reported disturbed sleep, physical discomfort, changes in eating, or less exercise. The high score on the reverse-coded physical scale reflected relatively little reported strain; it was not evidence of improved physical health.

These are participants’ reports of their experience, not proof that AI caused changes in health. Alongside the other readings, the study points to another evaluation question: whose experience disappears inside an average? Asking about persistence or willingness to use a tool should leave room to ask about discomfort and disruption as well.

The productive tension

What happens when a learner accepts less assistance?

Could a small pause before accepting an AI answer preserve effort without making a task unnecessarily frustrating?

Explore the tension

Could a small pause before accepting an AI answer preserve effort without making a task unnecessarily frustrating? The intention survey by Isaac Davor and colleagues, the dependency analysis by ShuFei Li and colleagues, and the wellbeing account by Michael Agyemang Adarkwah and colleagues suggest different outcomes to track in such a test. Measure willingness to continue, the reasoning a learner can explain independently, and reported strain separately. This is a proposed comparison; these papers do not establish that the pause would work.

Try it

Make one judgment visible

Before accepting an AI answer, write down one decision you still need to make yourself. Afterward, record the evidence for that decision and anything that remains uncertain.

An untested reflection prompt inspired by the questions above, not a proven attention intervention.

Can a learner explain the reasoning without the tool, and what effort or discomfort accompanied getting there?
