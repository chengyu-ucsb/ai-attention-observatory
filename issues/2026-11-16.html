Give AI learning tools a clear job

This week’s experiment and editorial point toward a practical question: which part of learning does an AI feature support, and what evidence would show that it helped?

An animated lesson can look impressive.

The opening argument

An animated lesson can look impressive. An AI tutor can make feedback immediate. Evaluating either requires a clear account of what learners are supposed to do. This short edition brings together a small instructional-video experiment and an editorial about self-regulated learning. Their contributions differ: one tests a presentation change; the other argues for keeping learners active in planning, monitoring, and revising their work.

In this edition

Theme 01

Adding motion did not establish a learning benefit

In Zhai’s video experiment, 40 undergraduates watched a 140-second lesson about woodworking joints, with either static images or manually animated versions. AI generated the base images; it did not generate the motion. The study found no statistically significant main effect of motion on a 10-item accuracy test. That does not establish that animation never helps: the sample was small and the lesson narrowly defined.

Theme 02

Give the learner decisions to make

Glick, Miedijensky, and Zhang’s editorial argues that AI should support learners in setting goals, evaluating feedback, and reflecting on their progress. Its discussion of earlier research spans planning support, feedback literacy, and anxiety about disclosing AI use. The useful design question is who keeps making judgments as assistance increases. This is the editorial’s synthesis of an existing collection, not a new intervention study or fresh evidence that a particular AI design works.

Theme 03

Keep experience and performance separate

Zhai measured perceived effort and reactions to the video alongside accuracy. Some exploratory comparisons varied across gender groups and presentation conditions. Those patterns do not establish inherent gender differences or justify assigning lessons by gender. The broader evaluation lesson is to ask separately whether people like a feature, how demanding they find it, and what they can actually do afterward.

The productive tension

Support can change the experience before it changes learning

The self-regulated learning editorial makes a case for preserving active judgment. The video experiment shows why a design feature needs a specific outcome test. Together, they motivate an evaluation question rather than a shared causal conclusion: does an AI feature help learners practice the process we care about? Neither source establishes lasting gains in independent learning from the particular tools discussed here.

Try it

Name the learning job

Pick an AI feature you use or teach with. Write down the action it should support, such as checking a claim or revising an explanation. Then choose an observable sign of success. Keep that sign separate from whether the tool feels helpful.
