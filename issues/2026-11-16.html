Who checks the answer?

An AI tutor can help a student practice, an adviser can change a decision, and a writing tool can hand the hard part to the next reader. Following the work reveals what each tool changes.

The opening argument

A student uses an AI companion to discuss a problem, then sits down to answer alone. A tenant uses AI to write a complaint, then an investigator has to work out what the tenant needs. In this edition

Theme 01

What can a student do when the tool is gone?

Stanford’s review of 52 research papers found mixed results. Using AI to study was generally more promising than asking it to complete an assignment, which sometimes left students learning less.

In Zhang’s classroom study, 126 students worked in groups with or without an AI companion. Students in the AI groups scored modestly higher on an individual test afterward. Researchers found no clear difference in how accurately students predicted their scores. In Zhai’s woodworking lesson, adding motion to AI-generated illustrations produced no detectable improvement on a ten-question quiz among 40 undergraduates. These studies tested different design choices and different lessons.

Theme 02

Which part of thinking does the learner keep?

Glick and colleagues argue that students need opportunities to set goals and judge feedback. Education Week gives a concrete example from a Harvard guide. A nine-year-old who tries to recall an answer gets practice retrieving it. A tool that supplies the answer can skip that step. The guide draws on developmental research and offers design advice.

What does working hard feel like with a tutor? Ennion and McLellan compared a human tutor, an AI tutor and ordinary internet search at an English college. On the second set of math problems, teenagers assigned the AI tutor rated the work harder and reported more effort than those assigned the human tutor. The study did not test whether that extra effort improved learning.

A survey by Yu and colleagues captures a different experience. Students who rated AI agents as more capable also reported less unnecessary mental effort and more confidence in their academic abilities. That one-time survey cannot tell us which came first.

Theme 03

Would you ask for advice or let the adviser act?

In Jones and colleagues’ preprint, people learned to rely more on an accurate AI adviser than an inaccurate one when they could see which earlier answers were right. Without that feedback they struggled to tell the advisers apart. In Park and colleagues’ scenarios, people chose no adviser in 12% of decisions about soliciting advice and 38% of decisions about letting an adviser act for them.

Maier and colleagues asked participants to divide a potential lottery prize between themselves and a charity. People rated AI advice more favorably than human advice, yet donation amounts did not differ significantly across AI, human and no-advice groups. All three studies are preprints.

Theme 04

Who gets to choose when help appears?

In amNewYork, Adam Daly reports that New York City council members asked whether schools could enable Google Search for students while turning off its AI summaries. Google says administrators can select services. The unresolved question was whether a school could select search and summaries separately.

In a Guardian opinion essay, Robin Kirman sees a benefit in giving teenagers access to a patient tutor, but asks when an always-available guide leaves them room to work through uncertainty alone. That is an argument about autonomy. The council letter and the essay supply no evidence about changes in students’ concentration.

The productive tension

One person’s shortcut can become someone else’s reading assignment

In letters to the Guardian, a housing complaints investigator describes searching long AI-assisted letters to figure out what tenants want. Two other writers argue that help drafting a complaint gives people a way to challenge institutions when they lack time or confidence. These firsthand accounts show why both ends of an exchange matter. The letters cannot tell us how often either experience occurs. In Park’s adviser study, participants could decline help in each scenario. Someone receiving an AI-written letter has much less say in how it lands on their desk. How would a service change if the person who has to read the output helped design it?

Try it

Try the same task from the other side

Write a three-sentence request about a fictional billing error. Ask an AI tool to expand it. Give both versions to someone who has not seen your instructions. Can they find what happened, what you need them to do and by when? Ask which words helped them find the answer.

Use a fictional example. This is a reading exercise.

A few useful terms

calibration
How closely a person’s estimate of their own performance matches what they actually do.
retrieval practice
Trying to bring information to mind before looking up the answer.
preprint
A publicly shared research paper that can change before journal publication.
